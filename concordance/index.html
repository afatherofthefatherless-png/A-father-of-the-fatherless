How "even" works in the King James Bible

What Strong's numbers are

James Strong was an American Bible scholar. In 1890 he published The Exhaustive Concordance of the Bible. It lists every word in the King James Bible and every verse where each word appears.

Strong gave each Hebrew and Greek word of the original a number. Hebrew and Aramaic words run from H1 to H8674. Greek words run from G1 to G5624. The same original word always carries the same number, wherever it appears.

This is why the numbers matter. You do not need to read Hebrew or Greek to follow a word back to its source. One English word can stand for several original words. One original word can be rendered by several English words. The number shows you which is which. Most Bible tools still use Strong's numbers today, more than a century later.

"Even" is a good test. It is a small word that does many jobs in the King James Bible. Each entry in this concordance answers two questions about it:

  1. Where does "even" come from? What stands behind it in the Hebrew or Greek.
  2. What does "even" do? The job it does in the English sentence.

The two answers are kept apart.

Part 1. Where "even" comes from

Ask one question: what word in the Hebrew or Greek manuscripts matches this "even"? There are three answers. Each has its own coloured tag in the concordance columns.

1. Added by the translators (nothing in the original)

No Hebrew or Greek word stands behind this particular "even". The translators added it because the Holy Spirit inspired them to, and it makes the English read correctly.

Because there is no original word, there is no Strong's number. The columns read "none: supplied by the translators" or "(blank: supplied word)".

  • Genesis 19:9, "the man, even Lot". The Hebrew names the man and then Lot. "Even" joins the two.

2. A prefix or suffix (a small piece fixed to a larger word)

Hebrew often fixes short pieces to the front or the end of a word. These pieces carry meaning, but they are not words on their own.

  • A prefix goes on the front. In Genesis 9:3, "even as the green herb have I given you all things", "even" comes from kĕ-, "like". In Genesis 13:3, "from the south even to Bethel", it comes from ve-, "and", fixed to 'ad to make ve'ad.
  • A suffix goes on the end. In Genesis 6:17, "I, even I, do bring a flood", the second "I" comes from the ending -ni on hinneni, "behold me".

This "even" also has no Strong's number of its own. Any number shown belongs to the main word it is fixed to. In Genesis 13:3 that is H5704, 'ad. The columns say "not supplied" and show that number.

In Genesis, 11 entries carry this tag: 10 prefixes and 1 suffix (Genesis 6:17, "I, even I").

3. A real source word (a whole word in the original)

A complete Hebrew or Greek word stands behind "even" and has its own Strong's number.

  • Genesis 10:19, "even unto Lasha". The word is 'ad (H5704), "as far as".
  • Genesis 20:5, "she, even she herself said". The word is gam (H1571), "also" or "even".
  • Genesis 19:1, "two angels to Sodom at even". The word is 'ereb (H6153), "evening". Here "even" is the old English noun for evening. It translates the Hebrew noun one for one.

A question mark after a Strong's number means the sources disagree about it.

Part 2. What "even" does in the sentence

Each entry gets one sense label. The label describes the English sentence only. Where two labels could fit, the one listed first wins.

There are eight labels. Genesis uses the first six. The last two wait for later books.

1. Range

"As far as" or "until."

"Even" marks how far a distance or a time reaches.

Genesis 13:3, "from the south even to Bethel".

The grammar. "Even" works with a preposition, such as "to" or "unto", to mark an end point. In Hebrew this is usually 'ad.

2. Comparison

"Like" or "as."

"Even" sets one thing beside another.

Genesis 13:10, the plain of Jordan was well watered, "even as the garden of the LORD".

The grammar. "Even as" is a comparison phrase. "Even" sharpens "as". Not just similar. Truly like it.

3. Repeated person

The same person, named twice.

A person is named or pointed to twice. The second time, "even" comes before it.

Genesis 6:17, "I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth".

Genesis 20:5, "she, even she herself said, He is my brother".

The grammar. The repeat is for emphasis. The speaker leans on the pronoun. It says: this one and no other.

A special case. In Genesis 27:34, Esau cries, "Bless me, even me also, O my father." He says it again in Genesis 27:38: "bless me, even me also, O my father. And Esau lifted up his voice, and wept."

The Hebrew is gam-'ani: gam (H1571), "also", and 'ani (H589), "I/me". Two things happen at once. "Also" compares Esau with Jacob: me too, like him. "Even" repeats "me" and makes the plea sharper and more desperate. For the source, the concordance lists "even" here as a supplied word. It is listed on its own because of what it does in the sentence.

4. Apposition

Restating or pinning down.

"Even" opens a phrase that restates what was just said, or makes it exact. It can do this in three ways.

It names who is meant.

Genesis 19:9, "the man, even Lot".

It gives a number.

Genesis 47:2, "even five men".

It says the thing again with more weight.

Genesis 49:22, "a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough".

The grammar. Apposition means two phrases stand side by side and point to the same thing. The second explains the first. In modern English we would say "namely" or "that is". This label replaces the old labels Identifier and Repetition.

5. Intensifier

Adding weight.

"Even" makes a statement stronger. It does not repeat a person, and it does not name a time.

Genesis 34:29, "spoiled even all that was in the house".

Genesis 37:18, "even before he came near unto them".

Genesis 42:28, "it is even in my sack".

The grammar. Here "even" is an adverb of emphasis. Take it out and the sentence still stands, but it loses its force. This is closest to how we use "even" today.

6. Time of day

Evening.

"Even" is the old English word for evening.

Genesis 19:1, "there came two angels to Sodom at even".

The grammar. This "even" is a noun, not an adverb. It does not compare or stress anything. It names a time of day. It is the only sense where "even" means time itself.

7. Level

Flat, equal, balanced. (Kept for later books.)

"Even" describes a thing as flat, level or equal. It says what something is like, not how strongly something is said.

Job 31:6, "Let me be weighed in an even balance, that God may know mine integrity."

Psalm 26:12, "My foot standeth in an even place".

Luke 19:44, "And shall lay thee even with the ground".

The grammar. This "even" is an adjective. It describes a noun, as in "an even balance" or "an even place". In Luke 19:44 it comes after the verb and says what state the city will be left in: flat with the ground.

8. Other

A holding place. (Kept for later books.)

"Other" holds any "even" that fits none of the seven labels above. It is not a meaning. It is a shelf for words still waiting for a proper home.

How it works. Each "Other" entry is reviewed at the end of each book. It then moves to the right label, or a new label is made for it. No Genesis entry needs it so far.

Grammar words used on this page

  • Prefix: a piece fixed to the front of a word.
  • Suffix: a piece fixed to the end of a word.
  • Pronoun: a word that stands for a person, such as I, me, she.
  • Adverb: a word that adds to the meaning of a verb, an adjective or a whole statement.
  • Adjective: a word that describes a noun, such as "even" in "an even balance".
  • Apposition: two phrases side by side that name the same thing.
  • Supplied word: a word the translators added that has no word behind it in the Hebrew or Greek.
  • Strong's number: the number James Strong gave each Hebrew (H) and Greek (G) word in his concordance.

The Hebrew is va'ani hinneni: the pronoun "I" (ani) is stated, then repeated in hinneni (hinneh H2009, "behold", plus -ni, "I"), so the "I" is doubled and no separate word means "even". The repetition makes God himself the subject: the flood is something he personally brings, not just an event. It slows the reader as judgment lands and centers the verse on God's own identity and agency. Robin's angle: God created it all, so even he, the very one you'd expect to spare it, is the one bringing the flood, driven to it despite infinite patience. This self-emphasis carries the same weight as the "even Christ" pattern of condescension. The two "I"s are different words: ani (H589), the ordinary flat grammatical first-person pronoun, and the -ni ending on hinneni (H2009), making "behold me" or "here am I." This quietly echoes Abraham's answer at the binding of Isaac ("hinneni," Genesis 22:1), the same self-offering posture turned here toward destruction instead of sacrifice. Two registers of "I": the plain grammatical one and the dramatic present-tense one.
