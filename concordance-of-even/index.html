How to read the concordance

This page is a study of the word “even” in the KJB. The 30 Genesis entries below are Checked by hand. The other 1,364 occurrences are Suggested (not yet checked) and are not listed here until they have been checked.

Each row is headed by a Location, written as the full book name and a chapter:verse, for example Genesis 6:17.

Designation is the decision about what “even” is in that verse. It has two parts, kept separate:

  • The sense — what “even” is doing in the English sentence (Apposition, Range, and so on).
  • The source type — where “even” comes from in the Hebrew or Greek: an added word, a prefix or suffix, or a word of its own.

The sense never describes the source, and the source type never stands in for the sense.

Source type: where “even” comes from

Three source types are used. The test is what, in the Hebrew or Greek, the “even” corresponds to: nothing, a small piece of a word, or a whole word.

  • Added word Nothing in the original. There is no Hebrew or Greek word, or piece of a word, behind “even”. The KJB translators put it in to make the English read well or to mark a restatement, so there is no Strong’s concordance number for it. Example: Genesis 19:9, “the man, even Lot”. In the Hebrew or Greek and Strong’s columns, these rows still read “none: supplied by the translators” and “(blank: supplied word)”; that is the older wording for the same source type.
  • Prefix/suffix A small piece fastened to a larger word. “Even” does not translate a word of its own. It comes from a short piece attached to the front of a word (a prefix) or to the end of it (a suffix). A prefix example is kĕ- (“like”) in Genesis 9:3, or the “and” (vav) joined to ve’ad in Genesis 13:3. A suffix example is Genesis 6:17, where the repeated “I” comes from the ending -ni on hinneni. Both kinds share one tag because they work the same way: the piece has no Strong’s concordance number of its own, and any number shown belongs to the main word it is attached to. Of the 11 Genesis entries with this tag, 10 are prefixes and 1 (Genesis 6:17) is a suffix.
  • Word A whole word in the original. A complete Hebrew or Greek word stands behind “even” and has its own Strong’s concordance number. Examples: ’ad H5704 in Genesis 10:19, and gam H1571 in Genesis 20:5. A question mark after a number means the sources disagree about it.

Strong’s-based concordances often do not give “even” a Strong’s number of its own. That is expected for added words and for many prefixes and suffixes. When a number is shown, it belongs to the Hebrew or Greek word, or to the main word a prefix or suffix is attached to.

Sense: what “even” is doing

Every entry gets one sense label, according to what “even” is doing in the verse. The label describes the English sentence only. Where “even” came from is the source type above. When two labels could fit, the one listed first is used. Genesis uses six kinds:

  • Range means “as far as” or “until”, the reach of a distance or a time (Genesis 13:3, “even to Bethel”).
  • Comparison means “like” or “as”, setting one thing beside another (Genesis 13:10, the plain of Jordan “even as the garden of the LORD”).
  • Repeated person means a person is named or pointed at twice, the second time with “even” (Genesis 6:17, “I, even I”; Genesis 20:5, “she, even she herself”). Genesis 27:34, “Bless me, even me also,” is the same sense: the “even” is an added word, and the comparison with Jacob is carried by the Hebrew gam H1571, “also”. The same wording returns in Genesis 27:38.
  • Apposition means “even” introduces a phrase that restates or pins down what was just said. It may name who or what is meant (Genesis 19:9, “the man, even Lot”), give a number (Genesis 47:2, “even five men”), or say the same thing again with more detail (Genesis 49:22, “a fruitful bough, even a fruitful bough”).

Repeated person and Apposition are close cousins. Both name the same person or thing a second time. Apposition does it with new words, to say which one (Genesis 19:9, “the man, even Lot”; Genesis 47:2, “some of his brethren, even five men”). Repeated person says the same person again for emphasis (Genesis 6:17, “I, even I”; Genesis 20:5, “she, even she herself”). In Genesis, every Apposition “even” is an added word, put in by the translators, while most Repeated person entries translate a real Hebrew word for emphasis, gam (“also, even”). Genesis 10:21, “even to him”, sits on the line between the two.

  • Intensifier means “even” adds weight to a statement without repeating a person or naming a time (Genesis 34:29, “spoiled even all that was in the house”; Genesis 37:18, “even before he came near unto them”; Genesis 42:28, “it is even in my sack”).
  • Time: of day is the old English word for evening itself (Genesis 19:1, “at even”). It is the only entry where “even” literally means time. The Hebrew noun behind it is ’ereb H6153, so the source type is Word.

Two more labels are reserved for later books. Level will mean the adjective or verb sense of flat or equal (for example “an even balance”). Other is a holding label for anything that fits nothing else, to be reviewed at the end of each book.

LORD and Lord

The verse text is the verified KJB wording. LORD and GOD, shown in small capitals, stand for God’s name (YHWH). Lord is the title. lord is a human master. They are different words and are never treated as the same. Example: Genesis 13:10, “even as the garden of the LORD”; Genesis 44:18, “Oh my lord” (Joseph, as a man).

Repeated personPrefix/suffixAnd, behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth, to destroy all flesh, wherein is the breath of life, from under heaven; and every thing that is in the earth shall die.הִנְנִי hinneni (hinneh + suffix -ni "I")H2009
The Hebrew is va'ani hinneni: the pronoun "I" (ani) is stated, then repeated in hinneni (hinneh H2009, "behold", plus -ni, "I"), so the "I" is doubled and no separate word means "even". The repetition makes God himself the subject: the flood is something he personally brings, not just an event. It slows the reader as judgment lands and centers the verse on God's own identity and agency. Robin's angle: God created it all, so even he, the very one you'd expect to spare it, is the one bringing the flood, driven to it despite infinite patience. This self-emphasis carries the same weight as the "even Christ" pattern of condescension. The two "I"s are different words: ani (H589), the ordinary flat grammatical first-person pronoun, and the -ni ending on hinneni (H2009), making "behold me" or "here am I." This quietly echoes Abraham's answer at the binding of Isaac ("hinneni," Genesis 22:1), the same self-offering posture turned here toward destruction instead of sacrifice. Two registers of "I": the plain grammatical one and the dramatic present-tense one.

ComparisonPrefix/suffixEvery moving thing that liveth shall be meat for you; even as the green herb have I given you all things.כְּ kĕ- "like" (prefix on יֶרֶק yereq)none (kĕ- prefix has no classic Strong's concordance; STEP H9004; BLB tags noun H3418)
"Even as" renders the comparison prefix kĕ-, "just like the green herb". The prefix has no standard Strong's concordance number. The "even as" is the Hebrew comparative prefix kaph (the letter k) bound directly to the noun yereq ("green"), with eseb ("herb", H6212) following, so the phrase means "like the green herb"; there is no standalone word for "like." Strong's concordance has no number for the bound prefix itself; the related standalone form is kmo (H3644). This is a common Hebrew grammatical pattern: the kaph prefix appears throughout the Old Testament doing exactly this likening job. English parallels it with the suffix "-like" (childlike, warlike, lifelike), the same trick at the opposite end of the word.

ComparisonPrefix/suffixHe was a mighty hunter before the LORD: wherefore it is said, Even as Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD.כְּ kĕ- "like" (prefix on נִמְרֹד Nimrod)none (kĕ- prefix has no classic Strong's concordance; STEP H9004; BLB tags noun H5248)

RangePrefix/suffixAnd he went on his journeys from the south even to Bethel, unto the place where his tent had been at the beginning, between Bethel and Hai;וְעַד ve'ad (vav "and" + 'ad "until, as far as"); not suppliedH5704

The translators render that pair as "even to," so "even" in effect carries the vav, and 'ad does the pointing toward Bethel. Sitting between "from the south" and "to Bethel," it stretches the journey description: the verse names the starting direction, then the end-point, and "even" gives the sense of going the whole distance, all the way back. The word 'ad (H5704) then appears again at "unto the place" (the KJB's "unto"), tying the end-point of the trip to the spot where his tent had been at the beginning.

ComparisonAdded wordAnd Lot lifted up his eyes, and beheld all the plain of Jordan, that it was well watered every where, before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, even as the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt, as thou comest unto Zoar.none: supplied by the translators(blank: supplied word)
The English "even" has no Hebrew word behind it. It is an English addition that makes the simile flow naturally, because Hebrew and English construct comparison differently; the Hebrew simply has the likening prefix kĕ- (kĕgan, "like a garden"). The verse stacks three comparisons at once: (1) the Jordan plain like the garden of the LORD, Eden, the ideal past; (2) like the land of Egypt, the present abundance Lot is leaving behind; (3) as thou comest unto Zoar, the near future he is heading toward. So the text presents this as the best-looking real estate in every direction that matters to Lot, making his choice feel inevitable, and setting up the irony that the most desirable spot on the map is also the one God is about to destroy.

Time: of dayWordAnd there came two angels to Sodom at even; and Lot sat in the gate of Sodom: and Lot seeing them rose up to meet them; and he bowed himself with his face toward the ground;עֶרֶב 'ereb (בָּעֶרֶב ba'erev, "in the evening")H6153
Here "even" is the old English noun for evening ("at even"), a time marker, not a comparison or an intensifier. This is a different job from Genesis 13:10 and 13:3. The Hebrew behind it is 'ereb (H6153), "evening," in ba'erev ("in the evening"), from the root ayin-resh-bet, which carries the sense of growing dark, the mixing of light and dark. Notable coincidence: English "even" (E-V-E-N) and Hebrew 'ereb (E-R-E-V) look and sound alike. Both are four letters in an E-_-E-_ frame and both contain a V (in the Hebrew the bet is pronounced v), and the other consonant differs. They are not etymologically related: English even comes from the Germanic aefen (Old English ǣfen), while 'ereb is Semitic. Still worth flagging as a surface resemblance.

Repeated personAdded wordAnd when Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with a great and exceeding bitter cry, and said unto his father, Bless me, even me also, O my father.none: supplied by the translators ("also" = גַּם gam H1571)(blank: supplied word)
Translator-added: the Hebrew is gam-'ani, "also me" (gam H1571, "also"; 'ani H589, "I/me"). The KJB renders gam as "also" and supplies "even" in front of it, so the "even" is the translators' sharpening of the plea, not a separate Hebrew word. It does two jobs at once. It is emphasis: Esau cried "with a great and exceeding bitter cry," and "even me" is that cry turned into words, pointed and almost desperate. It is also comparison: "also" is the additive word, so Esau is measuring himself against Jacob in the same scene, as if to say "me too, like him." The thing being compared is the speaker himself against another person, which is why this is its own sense: the "even" lifts the "also" from a plain addition into a self-comparison with the brother who just took the blessing. Esau repeats the same words in 27:38.
