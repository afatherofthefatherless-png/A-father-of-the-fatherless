Repeated personPrefix/suffixAnd, behold, I, even I, do bring a flood of waters upon the earth, to destroy all flesh, wherein is the breath of life, from under heaven; and every thing that is in the earth shall die.הִנְנִי hinneni (hinneh + suffix -ni "I")H2009
The Hebrew is va'ani hinneni: the pronoun "I" (ani) is stated, then repeated in hinneni (hinneh H2009, "behold", plus -ni, "I"), so the "I" is doubled and no separate word means "even". The repetition makes God himself the subject: the flood is something he personally brings, not just an event. It slows the reader as judgment lands and centers the verse on God's own identity and agency. Robin's angle: God created it all, so even he, the very one you'd expect to spare it, is the one bringing the flood, driven to it despite infinite patience. This self-emphasis carries the same weight as the "even Christ" pattern of condescension. The two "I"s are different words: ani (H589), the ordinary flat grammatical first-person pronoun, and the -ni ending on hinneni (H2009), making "behold me" or "here am I." This quietly echoes Abraham's answer at the binding of Isaac ("hinneni," Genesis 22:1), the same self-offering posture turned here toward destruction instead of sacrifice. Two registers of "I": the plain grammatical one and the dramatic present-tense one.

ComparisonPrefix/suffixEvery moving thing that liveth shall be meat for you; even as the green herb have I given you all things.כְּ kĕ- "like" (prefix on יֶרֶק yereq)none
"Even as" renders the comparison prefix kĕ-, "just like the green herb". The prefix has no standard Strong's concordance number. The "even as" is the Hebrew comparative prefix kaph (the letter k) bound directly to the noun yereq ("green"), with eseb ("herb", H6212) following, so the phrase means "like the green herb"; there is no standalone word for "like." Strong's concordance has no number for the bound prefix itself; the related standalone form is kĕmo (H3644). This is a common Hebrew grammatical pattern: the kaph prefix appears throughout the Old Testament doing exactly this likening job. English parallels it with the suffix "-like" (childlike, warlike, lifelike), the same trick at the opposite end of the word.

ComparisonPrefix/suffixHe was a mighty hunter before the LORD: wherefore it is said, Even as Nimrod the mighty hunter before the LORD.כְּ kĕ- "like" (prefix on נִמְרֹד Nimrod)none
"Even as Nimrod" renders kĕ-Nimrod, "like Nimrod", introducing a proverbial saying. This "even as" is comparison by archetype, not parallel situation. The text quotes a proverb people already repeated ("wherefore it is said"), and the kaph prefix on Nimrod's name signals he became the standard: when you want to describe a great hunter, you say "like Nimrod." Contrast with Genesis 9:3, where "even as" maps one gift pattern onto another (animals like green plants). Here the likeness is to a person who set the benchmark.

RangePrefix/suffixAnd he went on his journeys from the south even to Bethel, unto the place where his tent had been at the beginning, between Bethel and Hai;וְעַד vĕ'ad (vav "and" + 'ad "until, as far as"); not suppliedH5704
Unlike Genesis 13:10, this "even" is not a supplied word. The Hebrew is vĕ'ad: the conjunction "and" (vav) joined to 'ad (H5704), "until, as far as, up to." The translators render that pair as "even to," so "even" in effect carries the vav, and 'ad does the pointing toward Bethel. Sitting between "from the south" and "to Bethel," it stretches the journey description: the verse names the starting direction, then the end-point, and "even" gives the sense of going the whole distance, all the way back. The word 'ad (H5704) then appears again at "unto the place" (the KJB's "unto"), tying the end-point of the trip to the spot where his tent had been at the beginning.

ComparisonAdded wordAnd Lot lifted up his eyes, and beheld all the plain of Jordan, that it was well watered every where, before the LORD destroyed Sodom and Gomorrah, even as the garden of the LORD, like the land of Egypt, as thou comest unto Zoar.none: supplied by the translatorsnone
The English "even" has no Hebrew word behind it. It is an English addition that makes the simile flow naturally, because Hebrew and English construct comparison differently; the Hebrew simply has the likening prefix kĕ- (kĕgan, "like a garden"). The verse stacks three comparisons at once: (1) the Jordan plain like the garden of the LORD, Eden, the ideal past; (2) like the land of Egypt, the present abundance Lot is leaving behind; (3) as thou comest unto Zoar, the near future he is heading toward. So the text presents this as the best-looking real estate in every direction that matters to Lot, making his choice feel inevitable, and setting up the irony that the most desirable spot on the map is also the one God is about to destroy.

Time: of dayWhole wordAnd there came two angels to Sodom at even; and Lot sat in the gate of Sodom: and Lot seeing them rose up to meet them; and he bowed himself with his face toward the ground;עֶרֶב 'ereb (בָּעֶרֶב ba'ereb, "in the evening")H6153
Here "even" is the old English noun for evening ("at even"), a time marker, not a comparison or an intensifier. This is a different job from Genesis 13:10 and 13:3. The Hebrew behind it is 'ereb (H6153), "evening," in ba'ereb ("in the evening"), from the root ayin-resh-bet, which carries the sense of growing dark, the mixing of light and dark. Notable coincidence: English "even" (E-V-E-N) and Hebrew 'ereb (E-R-E-V) look and sound alike. Both are four letters in an E-_-E-_ frame and both contain a V (in the Hebrew the bet is pronounced v), and the other consonant differs. They are not etymologically related: English even comes from the Germanic aefen (Old English ǣfen), while 'ereb is Semitic. Still worth flagging as a surface resemblance.

Repeated personAdded wordAnd when Esau heard the words of his father, he cried with a great and exceeding bitter cry, and said unto his father, Bless me, even me also, O my father.none: supplied by the translators ("also" = גַּם gam H1571)none
Translator-added: the Hebrew is gam-'ani, "also me" (gam H1571, "also"; 'ani H589, "I/me"). The KJB renders gam as "also" and supplies "even" in front of it, so the "even" is the translators' sharpening of the plea, not a separate Hebrew word. It does two jobs at once. It is emphasis: Esau cried "with a great and exceeding bitter cry," and "even me" is that cry turned into words, pointed and almost desperate. It is also comparison: "also" is the additive word, so Esau is measuring himself against Jacob in the same scene, as if to say "me too, like him." The thing being compared is the speaker himself against another person, which is why this is its own sense: the "even" lifts the "also" from a plain addition into a self-comparison with the brother who just took the blessing. Esau repeats the same words in 27:38.
